Conversations with friends and family who do not have a technical background have made me more aware of something: the computers and AI systems we use every day can still feel strangely intangible. They are right in front of us, yet it can be difficult to picture how they work.

We send a message, upload a file, or ask AI to complete a task. A response soon appears on the screen. But what happens between those two moments? Where does the data go? Which services handle it? Who stores and processes it? Where, physically, is the thing we call “the cloud”?

Someone familiar with computing may be able to picture a rough path. For someone else, that space can be blank. Words such as “cloud,” “model,” and “agent” do little to fill it on their own.

## There is a physical world beyond the screen

That gap in understanding also makes me think about data centers. Using an online service does not necessarily bring to mind a distant building, its power supply, or its cooling equipment. There is little visible connection between an effortless click and resource use or everyday life somewhere else.

I would like to help make these questions more concrete. How does a data center work? Why does computing need energy and cooling? When nearby residents raise concerns about noise or resource use, which facilities and operating practices are involved? How do the answers differ across places and systems?

Answering those questions requires specific sources and attention to local conditions. What interests me is how to present that information so people without a technical background can understand and discuss it without first learning an entire engineering vocabulary.

## Understanding should not require becoming an expert

Nobody needs to master network protocols, model training, or data center engineering simply to use technology. But when a system handles someone's data, acts on their behalf, or affects their surroundings, they should have a chance to understand what is happening.

To me, being informed includes being able to ask questions. Why does this information need to be sent? Who can see it? If I disagree, do I have another option?

Those questions need a process that people can picture. When all a person can see is their input and the system's output, it is difficult to know where to ask questions or what they have actually agreed to.

## A “memory palace” for abstract processes

I think of this as helping people build a “memory palace” for technology. I borrow the phrase for a simple idea: giving abstract processes recognizable places, paths, and actions, so people have a mental framework they can return to when they encounter a similar situation.

Take a file leaving someone's device. Which parts go to an external service? Where might a copy remain? How does the result come back? At what point does an AI request become an operation that reads a file, sends information, or changes a record? Which steps can someone inspect, change, or stop?

A visualization might be a diagram, an animation, or a clear preview before an action. It can be simple. What matters is whether it helps someone connect the action they see with the process behind it.

That explanation also needs to be honest. What does an arrow stand for? Which steps were actually observed, and which are simplifications? A memorable account should leave room for uncertainty and differences between systems.

## Hidee is my first exploration

This is why I started exploring [Hidee](https://hidee.net/).

When we give an AI agent a task, we usually describe a goal, such as “help me organize these materials.” Pursuing that goal may involve reading files, calling external services, and sending some information outside the device. There is a lot to explain between that one sentence and the operations it sets in motion.

I want Hidee to help people without a technical background see what an agent is about to send, who will receive it, and where they can intervene. This is something I hope to improve over time, including checking whether those explanations make sense in actual use.

An AI agent's actions are not magic or wishes coming true. They depend on software, data, permissions, and external services. Making those processes visible can help people decide what information to share, which actions to allow, and when to stop.

I see this as work with a public benefit, worth pursuing over the long term. As technology develops quickly, I hope we also spend time helping more people understand it, with meaningful opportunities to be informed and to take part in decisions about its use.
